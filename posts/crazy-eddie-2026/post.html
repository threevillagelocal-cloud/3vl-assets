Crazy Eddie: His Prices Were INSANE!

Before Best Buy, before Amazon, Long Islanders bought their first VCR, boombox and big-screen TV from a guy on TV who shouted at them. This is the story of the Crazy Eddie on Route 347, the commercials everyone can still recite, and the scandal that brought it all down.

Our store

The Crazy Eddie on Route 347

If you grew up around Stony Brook, Setauket, Lake Grove or Smithtown in the 1980s, you remember the drive out Route 347 to Crazy Eddie. It was where you went for your first Walkman, the family's first VCR, a stack of blank cassettes and a car stereo. Walls of TVs, all on at once. Salesmen who would beat any price you could find. And that voice from the commercials still ringing in your head.

📍 1010 Smithtown Bypass (Route 347), Nesconset📅 Opened 1984📏 About 10,000 sq ft🔌 Closed 1989

Locals called it the Lake Grove store, the Smithtown store or the Nesconset store, depending on which way they drove in. Crazy Eddie's own 1985 annual report lists it at 1010 Smithtown Bypass, Nesconset, opened in 1984, with about 10,000 square feet of space.

Plenty of us remember it opening in 1985, and that's close: the company's records put it in 1984, which means it was likely already open when Crazy Eddie went public that September. It was one of the chain's first Long Island stores, along with Carle Place and Syosset.

ThenCrazy Eddie, 1010 Smithtown Bypass: TVs, VCRs, stereos, car audio, records and tapes.
NowTesla Smithtown, right next door at 1000 Nesconset Highway. Electronics never really left this stretch of 347.

How it started

From one Brooklyn shop to 43 stores

It began in 1969 as a single store called Sight and Sound at 1117 Kings Highway in Brooklyn, run by Eddie Antar, his father Sam M. Antar and a partner. In 1971 Eddie bought out the partner and renamed the store after the nickname customers had given him: Crazy Eddie.

The formula was simple and loud: name brands, rock-bottom prices, a promise to beat anyone's advertised price, and nonstop advertising. Stores spread across Long Island, Manhattan, New Jersey, Connecticut and Pennsylvania. When Crazy Eddie went public in September 1984 at $8 a share, it had 13 stores, three of them on Long Island. At its peak it ran 43 stores in four states and reported $352.5 million in yearly sales.

The genius marketing

“His prices are INSANE!”

Tap to watch a classic Crazy Eddie commercial.

The man screaming at you was Jerry Carroll, a disc jockey at WPIX-FM known as “Dr. Jerry.” Crazy Eddie's ad director, Larry Weiss, hired him in 1976, and the first TV spots ran during the late-night soap parody Mary Hartman, Mary Hartman. Over the next decade he starred in thousands of radio and TV ads: arms flailing, eyes bulging, standing in front of a wall of stereos and shouting the same unforgettable line.

“Shop around. Get the lowest sale prices you can find. Then see Crazy Eddie and he'll beat 'em!”

The promise in the commercials, as it appeared on screen

The ads were cheap to make, ran constantly, and became part of New York culture. Crazy Eddie held a “Christmas in August” sale every summer, Saturday Night Live spoofed it in 1977 with Dan Aykroyd as “Crazy Ernie,” and a Crazy Eddie commercial even shows up in the 1984 movie Splash. Ask anyone over 45 on Long Island to finish the line “His prices are...” and watch what happens.

Case file

The scandal

The crazy part was the books

Behind the cheap prices was one of the biggest securities frauds of the 1980s. Before the company went public, the family skimmed cash: they pocketed sales tax, kept cash sales off the books and paid some workers under the table. Bank records later showed almost $8 million in cash deposits between 1979 and 1983.

After the 1984 stock offering, the scheme flipped. Now the goal was to make the company look more profitable so the stock would keep climbing. Inventory was inflated by millions, outside money was booked as store sales, and the Antar family sold about $90 million of stock along the way.

In November 1987 a takeover group led by Elias Zinn ousted the Antars, opened the books and found about $65 million in inventory that wasn't there. The chain filed for bankruptcy in 1989, and by the end of that November every Crazy Eddie, including the store on Route 347, was closed.

Eddie Antar fled the country in February 1990. Israeli police arrested him in June 1992, living under the name David Cohen, and he was sent back in January 1993. After a conviction that was overturned on appeal, he pleaded guilty in 1996 and was sentenced in 1997 to 82 months in prison. His cousin Sam E. Antar, the company's former CFO, cooperated with prosecutors and went on to become a well-known fraud expert who now helps investigators catch schemes like the one he once helped run. Eddie Antar died in September 2016 at 68.

The spot today

Still the place for new tech

Drive Route 347 today and the old Crazy Eddie stretch is home to Tesla Smithtown at 1000 Nesconset Highway, which opened in fall 2020 in the former Sixth Avenue Electronics building. Some things don't change: almost 40 years later, this corner of Nesconset is still where people go to see the newest gadgets on wheels and off.
